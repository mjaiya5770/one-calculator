BTU Estimator

Most BTU calculators ask for square feet and stop. This one asks the questions that actually move the load — climate zone, insulation, sun, ceiling height, people, even the kitchen — and gives heating AND cooling, because a house has to survive both.

A first-pass sizing estimate — the honest kind, with its limits stated below. Final whole-house equipment sizing belongs to a Manual J load calculation; this gets you the right shortlist and exposes oversized quotes.

How it works

Cooling starts at 20 BTU per sq ft, then corrects: ceiling height adds 5% per foot over 8; insulation multiplies by 1.0 (good), 1.15 (average) or 1.35 (poor); sun exposure bends cooling by 0.9–1.12; every occupant beyond the first two adds 600 BTU; a kitchen adds 1,200. Heating is climate-driven: the factor runs 25 BTU/sq ft in zone 1 to 55 in zone 7, with the same ceiling and insulation corrections. Tonnage is cooling ÷ 12,000, reported as a decimal on purpose — real systems come in half-ton steps, and 2.88 tons means a 3-ton class unit, not a squeezed 2.5.

Estimates only — a genuine first pass. Duct losses, cathedral ceilings and unusual glass move real loads further; a Manual J accounts for them room by room. One warning worth the space: oversizing AC is worse than slight undersizing — short cycles, clammy air, higher bills. If a quote jumps two sizes above this estimate, ask what their J-load says.

Method & worked example

Method: Cooling = area × 20 BTU/sq ft × ceiling factor (1 + 5% per ft over 8) × insulation (good 1.0 / average 1.15 / poor 1.35) × sun (0.9 / 1.0 / 1.12) + 600 BTU per occupant past two + 1,200 for a kitchen. Heating = area × zone factor (25 zone 1 to 55 zone 7) × ceiling × insulation. Tons = cooling ÷ 12,000.

Worked example: A 1,500 sq ft average-insulated home, zone 4: 34,500 BTU cooling (2.88 tons) and 69,000 BTU heating.

When to use this

  • You are replacing an AC or furnace and a quote's tonnage smells oversized.
  • You are adding a mini-split to a room, addition or garage conversion.
  • You want heating AND cooling loads from one honest first pass.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the floor area being conditioned.
  2. Pick your climate zone for the heating load.
  3. Rate the insulation honestly — average is the honest default.
  4. Add sun exposure, ceiling height, occupants and kitchen, then read both loads.

Another worked example

A tough small case: 800 sq ft, 9-ft ceilings, zone 6, poor insulation, sunny exposure, 4 occupants and a kitchen. Cooling = 800 × 20 × 1.05 × 1.35 × 1.12 + 1,200 + 1,200 = 27,802 BTU (2.32 tons); heating = 800 × 50 × 1.05 × 1.35 = 56,700 BTU. Small footprint, serious loads — envelope beats area.

Frequently asked questions

How many BTU do I need for 1,500 sq ft?

Cooling: about 34,500 BTU (2.88 tons → a 3-ton class system) for an average-insulated home with 8-ft ceilings. Heating in climate zone 4: about 69,000 BTU. Same house, two different loads — in most of the country winter asks for more raw BTU than summer.

How many BTU for a 400 sq ft room?

About 9,200 BTU cooling at the average insulation factor — a 9,000–10,000 BTU window unit or mini-split head. A sunny west-facing room or a kitchen pushes toward the top of that range and beyond.

What is a ton of air conditioning?

12,000 BTU per hour — the cooling delivered by melting one ton of ice over a day, a unit old enough to be charming. Homes run 1.5–5 tons. Half-ton steps are standard: your decimal tonnage rounds to the nearest available size, with the oversizing warning above in mind.

Does insulation really change the size?

A poor envelope multiplies the load by 1.35 against 1.0 for a good one — on 1,500 sq ft that is the difference between roughly 2.5 and 3.4 tons of cooling. Sealing and insulation are one-time costs; the bigger machine bills you monthly for decades. An 800 sq ft drafty, sunny zone-6 space with a kitchen lands near 27,800 BTU cooling and 56,700 heating — small house, serious loads.

Mini-split or central?

Mini-splits shine for additions, single rooms and homes without ducts — and they zone beautifully. Central wins where ducts exist and whole-house evenness matters. The HVAC Cost Estimator prices both routes for your tonnage.

What size AC for a 2,000 sq ft house?

At the average settings here, roughly 46,000 BTU cooling (about 3.8 tons → a 4-ton class unit) in a mid climate — but zone, glass and insulation legitimately move the answer a full ton either way, which is why the inputs exist.

Does this replace a Manual J?

No — it is the screening estimate that tells you whether a quote's Manual J is plausible. For equipment purchase on a whole house, get the J-load; for room units, additions and quote-checking, this pass is the right tool.
