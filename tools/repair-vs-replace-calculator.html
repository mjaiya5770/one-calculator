Repair vs Replace Calculator

Every aging car, furnace and roof eventually asks the same question. This prices the repair in the only unit that matters — dollars per added year of life — sets it against the same unit for a replacement, and shows the classic 50% rule as the guideline it is, not a law.

Replacement cost should be net: the new item's price minus what the old one fetches as a trade-in or sale. "Value the repair adds back" matters for cars — a $2,800 transmission in a car worth $12,000 rarely adds $2,800 of resale value; $1,000 is a realistic default. The extra-running-cost line captures the old furnace's wasted gas or the old fridge's electricity appetite, per year of its added life.

How it works

Repair cost per added year = (repair quote + extra running cost × added years) ÷ added years. Replacement cost per year = net replacement cost ÷ replacement life. The cheaper unit cost wins the math verdict. The value-uplift input is reported separately — a repair that restores resale value effectively costs less if you sell soon after, but the uplift is speculative until a sale happens, so it never flatters the per-year figure here. The 50% rule — replace when a repair exceeds half the item's current value — is shown alongside as a guideline: it ignores how much life the repair buys, which is exactly the number that decides real cases. A repair at 60% of value that adds five years can be a bargain; one at 30% that adds six months can be a trap.

A decision aid, not a verdict on your specific item. It cannot see the repair's reliability (a rebuilt transmission vs a new one), safety questions, downtime cost, or the cascade risk that one repair reveals the next. If the item is safety-critical — brakes, structure, a failing roof over living space — weight those above any cost-per-year line.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Repair cost per added year = (repair quote + extra yearly running cost × added years) ÷ added years. Replacement cost per year = net replacement cost ÷ expected replacement life. The 50% rule compares the repair quote with half the item's current value — a guideline shown beside the math, not a substitute for it. Any resale value the repair adds back is reported separately, never netted into the per-year figure.

Worked example: Car example: $2,800 ÷ 3 years = $933 per added year vs $26,000 ÷ 10 = $2,600 per year for the replacement. The quote is 23.3% of the car's $12,000 value.

When to use this

  • A transmission, compressor or roof section fails and the quote is big enough to hurt
  • An appliance repairer is at the door and you need the decision framework now, not tonight
  • You are deciding whether an aging car earns one more repair or gets replaced
  • A home warranty company offers a repair and you want to know if cash-out-and-replace beats it

How to use it, step by step

  1. Pick a preset to load typical figures, or enter your item's numbers directly.
  2. Enter the repair quote in full — then consider running it again padded by 30%.
  3. Estimate the years the repair adds; use the warranty period if one is offered.
  4. Enter the net replacement cost (after trade-in) and its expected lifespan.
  5. Add any extra yearly running cost of keeping the old item (fuel, electricity, repeat callouts).
  6. Read both signals: cost per added year for the math, the 50% line for the smell test.

Frequently asked questions

What does the default car example say?

The $2,800 repair on a $12,000 car is 23.3% of value — comfortably under the 50% guideline — and at 3 added years it costs $933 per added year against $2,600 per year for the replacement. Repair wins clearly. The verdict flips when added life shrinks: at 1 added year the same repair costs $2,800 a year and loses.

Where does the 50% rule come from, and should I trust it?

It is a long-standing consumer heuristic: once a single repair passes half the item's value, the item is usually near end-of-life and further repairs tend to follow. Use it as a smell test. The cost-per-added-year math is the better tool because it prices what the repair actually buys you — time.

When is replacing better even if the repair is cheaper per year?

When reliability is the product: a work vehicle that cannot be down, a furnace in a cold climate, anything where failure costs more than the item. Also when the replacement is dramatically cheaper to run — an old heat pump replaced by a modern one can repay a chunk of its cost in energy savings, which the extra-running-cost line models.

How do I estimate "years of life the repair adds"?

Ask the repairer directly — a good shop will tell you whether the fix is a 1-year patch or a 5-year solution, and what else is likely to fail soon. For appliances, compare against typical lifespans: fridge 13 years, washer 10, water heater 8–12, roof 20–30 depending on material.

Should I count the money I have already spent on repairs?

No — that money is gone either way (the sunk-cost trap). Only future costs decide a future choice: the next repair quote, the remaining life each path buys, and the running costs ahead. Past repairs matter only as information about the item's condition.

🏠 Deciding on the house itself?

For big-ticket home systems, price the replacement properly first — roof, water heater and HVAC cost estimators use current planning ranges.

What if the repair comes with a warranty?

A warranty converts an uncertain added life into a guaranteed minimum — use the warranty period as your added-years floor and the repairer's estimate as the ceiling, then run the calculator at both ends. If repair wins even at the floor, it is a safe call.

How should I handle a repair quote that keeps growing?

Pad it. Discovery damage — the rot behind the wall, the second failing component — is the norm on older items. Run the decision at the quote and at quote + 30%; if the verdict flips between the two, get a firmer quote or lean replace.
