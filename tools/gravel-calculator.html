Gravel Calculator

How it works

Volume first: length × width × depth (in feet) gives cubic feet; divide by 27 for cubic yards. Tons = cubic yards × density — weight is how bulk yards actually price and deliver gravel. Bags divide the cubic feet by the bag size (0.5 cu ft is the common hardware-store bag). Costs are shown for both routes, because bags win on tiny jobs and lose badly on big ones.

Estimates assume a level, rectangular area and loose (uncompacted) depth. Gravel settles and compacts roughly 10–15% under traffic — order a little extra for driveways rather than a little less.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Volume = length × width × depth in feet. Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27. Tons = cubic yards × density (about 1.4 tons per yard for crushed stone). Bags = cubic feet ÷ bag size (0.5 cu ft for the standard hardware-store bag).

Worked example: A 20 × 10 ft driveway area at 3 in deep is 50 cu ft: 1.85 cubic yards, 2.59 tons, or 100 bags. Bulk at $40 a yard costs about $74.07 — the same gravel in $5 bags would run $500.

When to use this

  • You are topping up a driveway and the yard quotes by the ton while your tape measure works in feet and inches.
  • A path or drainage bed needs a number before you decide between a bulk drop and a stack of bags.
  • You are building a shed base and want the stone priced both ways on the same volume.
  • A supplier's minimum delivery is one yard and you need to know whether your job even clears it.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Measure the length and width of the area in feet.
  2. Set the depth in inches for this layer — run the calculator once per layer on a layered driveway build.
  3. Adjust the density if your stone is not typical crushed rock.
  4. Enter your local bulk price per cubic yard and the bag price.
  5. Read yards, tons and bags together, and compare the two cost lines before choosing how it arrives.

Another worked example

A 30 by 12 ft driveway section at 4 inches deep takes 120 cubic feet of stone: 4.44 cubic yards, weighing 6.22 tons at the standard 1.4 density. As a bulk load at $45 a yard that is $200. In 0.5 cu ft bags the same volume is 240 bags, and at $5.50 a bag the total is $1,320 — a $1,120 argument for the dump truck on anything driveway-sized.

Frequently asked questions

How much gravel for a 20 × 10 ft area, 3 inches deep?

That is 50 cubic feet: 1.85 cubic yards, about 2.59 tons at standard density, or 100 of the 0.5 cu ft bags. Bulk at $40 a yard costs about $74 — the same coverage in $5 bags runs $500, which is why bags are for patch jobs, not driveways.

How many tons are in a cubic yard of gravel?

About 1.4 tons for typical crushed stone — a cubic yard is 27 cubic feet of rock, and rock is heavy. Pea gravel is nearer 1.3 tons, dense graded base up to 1.6. When a supplier quotes by the ton, this conversion is the whole ballgame.

How deep should driveway gravel be?

In layers: 4–6 inches of large base stone, 2–3 of medium, 2–3 of fine top stone on a proper build — 8–12 inches total. This calculator works per layer; run it once for each with that layer’s depth.

How much extra should I order?

About 10% over the computed volume covers uneven ground, spillage and compaction. For a single delivery, rounding up is cheap insurance: a second small delivery usually costs more in fees than the extra half-yard would have.

What if my area is L-shaped or curved?

Break it into rectangles on paper — a main run plus the parking pad, or the path plus the widening at the gate — run the calculator for each with the same depth, and add the cubic yards and tons. For a curve, measure it as the rectangle it mostly fills and accept a small overage; gravel forgives surplus far better than a bare strip at the far end.

My bags are not 0.5 cu ft — how do I use the bag count?

The bag line in this calculator is built on the standard 0.5 cu ft hardware-store bag. If your bag is a different size, take the cubic feet figure from the results and divide it by your bag's cubic feet instead. The volume and tonnage do not change — only how many trips down the aisle they turn into.
