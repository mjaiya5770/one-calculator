Framing Estimator

Framing a wall is a counting problem with three traps: the stud at the end everyone forgets, the extra studs corners quietly eat, and the kings and jacks hiding in every doorway. Enter the wall and this estimator counts all three.

Counts one wall. For a room, run each wall and add the stud counts — corners are shared, so a four-wall room needs the corner allowance on two walls, not four.

Tip: dimensions accept tape-measure entries — try 6' 8 5/16" or 6.5.

How it works

Field studs: divide the wall length by the spacing and round up to whole spaces, then add one — a 16-ft wall at 16-in centres has 12 spaces and 13 field studs; the +1 is the end stud every rough guess misses. Corners and intersections add 2 studs each (the nailing surface for the adjoining wall and drywall). Each door or window opening adds about 4 — a king and jack on both sides, with the header stock assumed cut from the same pile. Plates are simple lineal feet: wall length × 2 runs (single top) or × 3 (double top). A 10% cull allowance covers the studs that arrive looking like bananas.

Estimates only. Headers over wide openings, fire blocking, and shear-wall nailing schedules come from your plans and local code, not from a count. When in doubt, the extra studs are the cheapest insurance on the job — lumber yards take returns.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Field studs = ceil(wall length × 12 ÷ spacing) + 1 end stud. Add 2 studs per corner/intersection and 4 per door or window opening, then the waste allowance. Plates = wall length × plate runs (3 for the standard double top plate).

Worked example: A 16-ft wall at 16-in centres with 2 corners and 1 doorway: 13 field studs + 8 corner/opening studs = 21, so 24 to buy with 10% waste — plus 48 lineal feet of plate.

When to use this

  • You are framing a partition, basement wall or shed and ordering studs.
  • You want to verify a lumber list before the yard delivers it.
  • You are comparing 16-in and 24-in spacing on a long wall run.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the wall length in feet.
  2. Choose spacing — 16 in standard, 24 in for light interior work.
  3. Count corners/intersections and openings honestly.
  4. Pick plate runs (3 = double top plate) and order the stud count shown.

Another worked example

A 24-ft basement wall at 24-in centres with 2 corners and 2 openings (a door and an egress window): 12 spaces + 1 = 13 field studs, + 12 corner/opening studs = 25, so 28 to buy with the cull allowance — and 72 lineal feet of plate. Wide spacing saved nothing here because the openings ate the difference.

Frequently asked questions

How many studs do I need for a 16-ft wall?

At 16-in centres: 12 spaces + 1 end stud = 13 field studs; two corners add 4 and one doorway adds 4 — 21 before waste, 24 to buy with a 10% cull allowance. Plus 48 lineal feet of plate (three 16-ft runs).

How many studs for a 24-ft wall at 24-in centres?

Also 12 spaces — spacing and length trade off. With 13 field studs, 2 corners and 2 openings the count is 28 to buy, and plates run 72 lineal feet. Wide spacing saves studs only on long, opening-free walls.

16 or 24 in on-centre?

Sixteen is the default for load-bearing and tiled walls and matches most drywall and sheathing layouts. Twenty-four (advanced framing) is code-legal for many single-story and interior walls and leaves more room for insulation — but check your plans before saving the lumber.

What size studs?

2×4 for most interior and single-story exterior walls; 2×6 for plumbing walls, tall walls, and exterior walls where deeper insulation pays. This estimator counts pieces — the Board Foot Estimator can price the pile either way.

Do I really need a waste allowance?

Yes — roughly one stud in ten in a typical lift is twisted, crowned badly or split. Pros order the allowance and cull on site; running short mid-wall costs a trip and a mismatched second lift.

How do I handle a wall longer than my studs are tall?

This estimator counts studs for standard 8–9 ft walls. For tall walls, order the next stud length up (10 or 12 ft) — the count stays the same, the board footage and price per piece rise.

Should plates match the stud size?

Yes — plates are the same dimension as the studs (2×4 wall, 2×4 plates; 2×6 wall, 2×6 plates), usually in the longest lengths the yard stocks to minimise joints.
