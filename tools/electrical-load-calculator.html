Electrical Load Calculator

Before the EV charger, the hot tub or the addition: what does the code’s own math say your house already draws? This is the NEC 220.82 optional method, step by step — general load, demand factor, HVAC, total amps, and the headroom left on your panel.

Method: NEC 220.82 (optional method for dwellings) — 3 VA per sq ft of living area, 1,500 VA per small-appliance circuit, 1,500 VA laundry, plus nameplate VA of fixed appliances. The first 10 kVA of that general load counts at 100%, the rest at 40%. The larger of AC or heating then adds at 100%. Fixed-appliance default of 20,000 VA is a typical all-electric bundle (range 8,000 + dryer 5,000 + water heater 4,500 + dishwasher 1,500 + disposal/fans 1,000) — replace it with your nameplates for a real answer.

How it works

Step 1, general load: living area × 3 VA + small-appliance circuits × 1,500 VA + laundry 1,500 VA + fixed appliances at nameplate. Step 2, demand factor: the code knows not everything runs at once — first 10,000 VA at 100%, remainder at 40%. Step 3, HVAC: the larger of air conditioning or electric heat at 100% (they do not run together, so never both). Total VA ÷ 240 V = calculated amps. Spare capacity = panel rating − calculated load: the honest headroom for the EV charger conversation, stated in amps instead of vibes.

This is the code’s calculation method for planning — it is not a permit, a load letter, or a substitute for an electrician, and utilities/AHJs may require the standard method (220.40s) or metered demand data instead. The continuous-load 80% convention applies to individual branch circuits and EV charging, not to this service calculation — do not double-derate. Panels also fill physically: spare amps in a panel with no free breaker spaces still means a subpanel.

Formula & worked example

Formula: NEC 220.82 optional method: general load = 3 VA/sq ft + 1,500 VA per small-appliance circuit + 1,500 VA laundry + fixed appliances at nameplate. Demand = first 10,000 VA at 100% + remainder at 40%. Add the larger of AC or electric heat at 100%. Amps = total VA ÷ 240. Spare = panel amps − calculated amps.

Worked example: 2,000 sq ft, 20,000 VA of appliances, 3,500 VA AC: general load 30,500 VA → demand 18,200 + 3,500 = 21,700 VA = 90.4 A — about 110 A spare on a 200 A panel.

When to use this

  • You are planning an EV charger, hot tub, addition or electric range.
  • An electrician quoted a service upgrade and you want the code math first.
  • You are buying a house and want to know what its panel can grow into.
  • You are comparing load management against a bigger service.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter living area, small-appliance circuits, and laundry.
  2. Total your fixed appliances from nameplates (VA ≈ watts for resistance loads) and enter the sum.
  3. Enter AC and electric heat nameplates — only the larger counts.
  4. Pick your panel size and read calculated amps and the spare headroom.

Another worked example

A 1,200 sq ft home, 13,700 VA of fixed appliances, 2,500 VA AC but 9,000 VA of electric heat, on a 100 A panel: general load 21,800 VA, demand-factored to 14,720, plus heat at 100% = 23,720 VA = 98.8 A. It fits — with 1.2 A of calculated headroom, which is to say it does not fit anything new.

Frequently asked questions

What load does a 2,000 sq ft all-electric house calculate to?

General load: 6,000 (area) + 3,000 (two small-appliance circuits) + 1,500 (laundry) + 20,000 (fixed appliances) = 30,500 VA. Demand: 10,000 + 40% × 20,500 = 18,200 VA. Plus AC 3,500 at 100% = 21,700 VA ÷ 240 = 90.4 A calculated load. On a 200 A panel that leaves about 110 A of calculated headroom — plenty for an EV charger by this method.

Can a 100 A panel take an EV charger?

The worked second example: a 1,200 sq ft home with a smaller appliance bundle calculates to 21,800 VA general, demand-factored plus the larger of AC/heat (9,000 VA electric heat) to 23,720 VA = 98.8 A — technically under 100 A, with 1.2 A to spare. Adding a 40 A EV circuit does not fit this calculation: that house needs load management, a smaller charger setting, or a service upgrade. This is precisely the question 220.82 exists to answer before anyone buys anything.

Why is only part of the load counted at 100%?

Diversity. Not every receptacle, light and appliance in a home runs at once — decades of metered data sit behind the demand factors. The first 10 kVA at 100% covers the always-on core; the 40% remainder reflects realistic coincidence. HVAC skips the discount because when it runs, it runs at full nameplate.

What is the difference between this and the standard method?

The optional method (220.82) is simpler and usually yields a lower calculated load; it is permitted for dwellings with a single service of 100 A+ where the AHJ accepts it. The standard method applies appliance-by-appliance demand tables. Either can govern a permit — your electrician and inspector decide which one your project files under.

Does spare capacity here mean I can add a 60 A breaker?

It means the service calculation has room. The breaker itself must still be sized to the load (a 48 A EV charger on a 60 A breaker is a continuous load at 125%), the panel needs physical spaces and bus rating for it, and the AHJ has the final word. Calculated headroom is the start of the conversation, not the permit.

My bill shows peak demand way below this. Who is right?

Both — they answer different questions. Metered peak is what your house did; 220.82 is what the code assumes it could do with nameplate loads and diversity. Many utilities accept 12 months of meter data (NEC 220.87) as an alternative basis for additions. If your metered peak is 40 A, that path may save a service upgrade.

What about a hot tub or a workshop?

Hot tubs enter as fixed appliances at nameplate (typically 5,500–11,000 VA with the heater) — add yours to the appliance total. Workshop welders, kilns and compressors likewise. Then watch the calculated load: those single big nameplates move the answer more than everything else in the house combined.
