How it works

New salary = current salary × (1 + raise % ÷ 100). Per-paycheck amounts divide by your pay frequency (26 for biweekly, 12 for monthly, etc.), and the hourly equivalent assumes a standard 2,080-hour work year. All figures are gross, before taxes and deductions.

These are gross figures. Your actual take-home increase will be smaller after federal, state, and FICA taxes.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Raise amount = current salary × raise %. New salary = current salary + raise amount. Gross per paycheck = new salary ÷ paychecks per year, and the hourly equivalent = new salary ÷ 2,080 hours.

Worked example: A 5% raise on $60,000 adds $3,000, for a new salary of $63,000 — that is $2,423.08 per biweekly paycheck ($115.38 more per check), or $30.29 an hour.

When to use this

  • Your review is coming and you want a rumoured percentage turned into an actual paycheck number before the meeting.
  • Two offers differ in salary and you want them compared per month, not just per year.
  • A cost-of-living adjustment was announced and you need to know if it keeps up with your rent increase.
  • You are hourly-minded and want a salaried raise expressed as an hourly equivalent to judge it fairly.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter your current annual salary, gross, from your offer letter or last payslip summary.
  2. Enter the raise as a percentage — if you were quoted a dollar amount, divide it by your salary and multiply by 100 to get the percent.
  3. Pick your pay frequency so the per-paycheck lines match how you are actually paid.
  4. Read the new salary, the extra per paycheck, and the hourly equivalent (the tool divides by a 2,080-hour work year).
  5. Remember every figure here is gross; the take-home increase will be smaller after tax and deductions.

A $54,000 salary with a 7% raise, paid monthly: the raise adds $3,780 a year for a new salary of $57,780. That is $4,815.00 per monthly paycheck, which is $315.00 more per check than before, and the new salary works out to $27.78 an hour on the tool's 2,080-hour year — a useful way to see that a solid-looking percentage is about three hundred a month, not a lifestyle change.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good raise percentage?

In the US, merit raises average 3–5% per year. Promotions or job changes often bring 10–20%. Always compare against inflation so your real pay isn't shrinking.

Should I negotiate a 3% raise?

If inflation is 3% and your raise is 3%, your purchasing power didn't change. Aim above inflation and back it up with your contributions.

Is a bonus better than a raise?

A raise compounds year after year; a one-time bonus doesn't. A $3,000 raise beats a $3,000 bonus in every future year.

My raise was quoted as a dollar amount, not a percent — how do I use this?

Divide the dollar raise by your current salary and multiply by 100. A $2,000 raise on a $54,000 salary is about 3.7%, which is the number to enter here — and the honest way to compare a flat-dollar offer against a percentage offer from another employer.

Why is the extra money in my first raised paycheck smaller than expected?

Raises usually take effect partway through a pay period, so the first check blends old-rate days and new-rate days. Benefit deductions that scale with pay, such as a percentage-based retirement contribution, also rise with the salary, trimming the visible increase further.

Does a raise change what my overtime is worth?

For hourly staff, yes directly: overtime is normally paid at one and a half times the new hourly rate, so a raise lifts every overtime hour too. For salaried staff this tool shows the hourly equivalent of the new salary (salary divided by 2,080), which is the figure to use when comparing against an hourly offer.
