Closing Cost Estimator

Closing costs arrive as one scary number at the end of a home purchase. This breaks it into the actual line items — lender fees, title, government charges, prepaids and escrows — each with its typical range, and uses your state's real average tax rate to size the tax escrow.

Fee ranges are typical US planning ranges (2026): origination 0.5–1% of the loan, title work 0.4–0.8% of the price, flat fees at customary levels. They are labeled estimates — your Loan Estimate document, which a lender must give you within three business days of application, is the real number for your deal. Escrows are not fees: they are your own future tax and insurance bills collected early, and they are the biggest single chunk for most buyers.

How it works

Lender fees scale with the loan (origination 0.5–1%, plus flat processing/underwriting). Title and settlement scale with the price. Government charges (recording, transfer tax where a state levies it) are small but real. Prepaids are timing, not profit: roughly 15 days of interest from closing to month-end, plus escrow deposits — property tax priced from the selected state's average effective rate (Tax Foundation Facts & Figures 2026) × the home price, and insurance — so the lender can pay those bills when they come due. The low/mid/high columns move every fee to its range end while keeping escrows identical, because escrows do not negotiate.

Estimates for planning only — not a Loan Estimate and not financial advice. Attorney states (much of the Northeast) add attorney fees; some states levy transfer taxes well above the 0.1% placeholder; discount points (not included) add 1% of the loan per point if you buy the rate down. Sellers sometimes credit closing costs — negotiate that in the offer, not at the closing table.

Method & worked example

Method: Closing costs are a schedule, not a formula: lender fees (origination as a percentage of the loan, flat processing), third-party fees (appraisal, credit, title as a percentage of price, settlement), government charges (recording, transfer tax), prepaids (about 15 days of interest), and escrow deposits — property tax at the selected state's average effective rate × price × months ÷ 12, plus insurance months. Fees are shown low/mid/high across their typical ranges; escrows are identical in all three columns because they are your own bills collected early, not charges.

Worked example: Default $400,000 loan on a $500,000 Texas home: mid total $14,288 (3.57% of the loan), of which about $4,100 is escrow deposits rather than fees.

When to use this

  • You are saving for a purchase and need the cash-to-close figure, not just the down payment
  • A lender's 'total closing costs' sounds high and you want an itemized second opinion
  • You are comparing two loan offers and need to separate real fees from escrow padding
  • You are choosing between states or counties and want the tax escrow impact priced in

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the loan amount and purchase price from your contract.
  2. Pick your state so the tax escrow uses its real average rate.
  3. Enter your insurance quote and the escrow months your lender requires.
  4. Read the fee lines (which negotiate) separately from the escrow lines (which do not).
  5. Compare the low/mid/high totals against any lender quote you receive.
  6. Take the itemized list to your Loan Estimate and challenge any line that is out of range.

Another worked example

A $640,000 loan on an $800,000 New Jersey purchase: the tax line dominates. At New Jersey's 1.88% average rate the home carries about $15,040 a year of tax, so six months of tax escrow alone is $7,520; origination at the 0.75% midpoint is $4,800, title $4,400, prepaid interest about $1,710 — the mid total lands near $22,640, roughly $8,300 more than the Texas example at the same price, driven almost entirely by the tax escrow.

Frequently asked questions

How much are closing costs on a $400,000 loan?

The mid estimate here is about $14,288 all-in — but roughly $4,100 of that is escrow deposits (your own tax and insurance money), so true fees are closer to $10,200, or about 2.5% of the loan. The commonly quoted "2–5% of the loan" band includes escrows, which is why it is so wide.

Which closing costs can I negotiate or shop?

Origination and lender processing fees are negotiable, and you can shop title, settlement and survey services (your Loan Estimate marks which ones). Government recording charges, transfer taxes, prepaid interest and escrow amounts are not negotiable — they are the same at every lender, which is a useful tell when comparing quotes.

Why do lenders collect so much tax escrow up front?

Federal rules let lenders keep up to a two-month cushion plus the months needed so the escrow account never goes negative before the tax bill lands. In a high-tax state the tax escrow alone can exceed every lender fee combined — in Texas at the 1.40% average rate, six months on a $500,000 home is $3,500.

What is the difference between closing costs and prepaids?

Closing costs are gone forever — fees for the loan, title and government charges. Prepaids and escrows are your own money moved earlier: interest for the partial first month, a year of insurance paid up front, and deposits toward future tax bills. Separating them (this estimator does) shows what a lender is actually charging you versus simply collecting.

Can closing costs be rolled into the loan?

Often, either by increasing the loan amount (if equity allows) or by taking a slightly higher rate for a lender credit. Neither makes them free — you pay interest on the rolled amount for years. Compare the lender-credit offer against cash-at-closing using the total-interest line of the Mortgage Calculator.

What is a Loan Estimate and how does it relate to this?

The Loan Estimate is the lender's official three-page disclosure, due within three business days of your application, using standardized boxes so quotes can be compared line by line. This estimator prepares you to read it — and to spot a quote whose 'fees' quietly include big escrows or points.

Can the seller pay my closing costs?

Often, yes — a seller credit negotiated into the purchase offer, subject to loan-program caps on concessions. It is effectively financed by the price, so it is not free money, but for cash-tight buyers it is a standard and legitimate structure.
