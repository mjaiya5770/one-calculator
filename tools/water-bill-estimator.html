Water Bill Estimator

Water bills confuse everyone because the unit — "per 1,000 gallons, combined water and sewer" — hides the usage. This estimator rebuilds yours fixture by fixture: showers, toilets, laundry, dishes, faucets — so you can see which habit is actually on the bill.

Find your combined rate on a recent bill: (water charge + sewer charge) ÷ usage in thousands of gallons. US utilities commonly land between $7 and $15 per 1,000 gal combined — sewer is often the bigger half.

How it works

Each fixture gets its planning rate: showers at 2.1 gallons per minute; toilets at 5 flushes per person per day times your flush volume; laundry at 30 gallons per load (modern machine average); kitchen/dishwasher share at 4 gal per person per day; faucets at 5. The default four-person home totals 156.63 gal/day indoors — 4,768 gallons a month — and showers are the biggest single line (67.2 gal/day), ahead of toilets (32). The bill is gallons ÷ 1,000 × your combined rate; fixed service charges sit on top at most utilities and are the part your habits cannot change.

Estimates only, for the usage-based portion of the bill. Tiered pricing (cheap first block, pricey later blocks) and drought surcharges bend the simple rate math; your utility's rate card is the final word. A bill far above this estimate for your household is the classic silent-leak signal — see the FAQ.

Method & worked example

Method: Daily gallons built fixture by fixture: showers (showers/day × minutes × 2.1 gpm) + toilets (people × 5 flushes × flush volume) + laundry (loads/wk ÷ 7 × 30 gal) + kitchen/dishes (people × 4) + faucets (people × 5), plus outdoor use and any leak allowance. Month = daily × 30.44; bill = gallons ÷ 1,000 × combined rate.

Worked example: A four-person home at standard fixtures: 156.63 gal/day indoors (showers 67.2, toilets 32, laundry 21.4, dishes 16, faucets 20) = 4,768 gal/mo — $57.21 at a $12 combined rate, $686.56 a year.

When to use this

  • Your water bill jumped and you need to know if usage explains it.
  • You are budgeting utilities for a new home or a growing household.
  • You want to know which fixture change actually moves the bill.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter household size and daily showers.
  2. Set shower length and your toilet's flush volume (check the tank stamp).
  3. Enter laundry loads per week and any outdoor use.
  4. Find your combined rate on a bill and enter it — then read the fixture breakdown.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average water bill for a family of 4?

Built fixture by fixture at standard rates: about 4,768 gallons a month — $57.21 at a $12 combined rate, before fixed service charges. National surveys put typical family water-plus-sewer bills in the $60–$110 band once fees are in. A frugal two-person home (3 laundry loads a week) lands near 2,449 gallons — $29.39 at the same rate.

Where does the water actually go?

In the default home: showers 67.2 gal/day (43%), toilets 32, laundry 21.4, faucets 20, kitchen/dishes 16. The shower is the bill. Two minutes off four daily showers saves about 500 gallons a month — $6 at $12 rates, every month, forever.

Why is my bill higher than this estimate?

Three usual suspects: a running toilet (up to 200 gal/day — $72/mo at $12 rates, silent and invisible — try the leak allowance at 100% to see it), irrigation beyond what you entered, or a tiered rate where your top block costs double the average. The dye-tablet toilet test takes five minutes and finds the first.

How much water does lawn watering use?

About 1,000 gallons per hour for a typical hose sprinkler — enter it as roughly 33 gal/day for a one-hour weekly session averaged out. A summer of Saturday watering outweighs every shower in the house.

Old toilets really use that much?

A pre-1994 toilet at 3.5 gal per flush, flushed 20 times a day by a family of four, uses 70 gal/day against 32 for a 1.6-gal model — about $16 a month at $12 rates. It is the single largest fixture swap in the house.

Why is sewer charged on water usage?

Because sewer flow is rarely metered — the water meter is the proxy. That is why summer watering inflates the sewer line too, and why many towns offer irrigation meters or winter-average sewer billing.

What uses more water: baths or showers?

A standard tub bath is 30–40 gallons; an 8-minute shower at 2.1 gpm is about 17. Showers win unless they stretch past 15–20 minutes, at which point the tub was the honest choice.
