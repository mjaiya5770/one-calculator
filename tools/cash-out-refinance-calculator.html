Cash-Out Refinance Calculator

Cash-out refinancing trades home equity for cash and a brand-new loan clock. Enter your current loan and the new one you are considering — see the new payment, how much extra interest the reset really costs, and whether there is a break-even at all.

The trap this calculator exposes: refinancing restarts the amortization clock, so even a lower rate can mean more total interest — and cash out means paying interest on the cash for up to 30 years. Compare the total-interest lines, not just the monthly payment. The Closing Cost Estimator itemizes what that closing % typically contains.

How it works

New loan = current balance + cash out. Both payments use the standard amortization formula on their own balance, rate and remaining term. Total interest is payment × months − balance for each loan. Break-even months = closing costs ÷ monthly payment saving — and when the new payment is higher, there is no break-even: the refinance is a purchase of cash and time, priced by the interest-delta line. The equity check shows your loan-to-value after the cash out; most lenders cap cash-out refinances near 80% LTV on a primary home.

Estimates only — not financial advice. Actual approval, rates and cash-out limits depend on credit, income, appraisal and lender rules. Interest on cash-out proceeds is generally only deductible when the money funds improvements to the home; check your situation with a tax professional.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Payment = balance × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), with r = annual rate ÷ 12 and n = months. Total interest = payment × n − balance. Break-even months = closing costs ÷ (old payment − new payment), defined only when the payment falls.

Worked example: Default: $280,000 at 5.9% with 22 years left pays $1,896.10/month and $220,570 of remaining interest. The $320,000 cash-out loan at 6.25% over 30 years pays $1,970.30 and $389,306 of interest — no break-even, because the payment rises.

When to use this

  • A lender mails you a cash-out offer and you want the unbiased total cost
  • You are funding a major renovation and comparing ways to borrow against the house
  • You are consolidating high-rate debt and need to see the true trade over the full term
  • Rates have dropped since you bought and the cash is a bonus on a refi you wanted anyway

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter your current balance, rate and years remaining from your statement.
  2. Enter the cash amount you want and the new loan's rate and term.
  3. Set closing costs (2% is a fair mid estimate; itemize it if you have a quote).
  4. Read the payment change AND the total-interest change — both, always.
  5. Check the break-even months against how long you will realistically stay.
  6. Compare the second-lien alternative (HELOC / home equity loan) before signing.

Another worked example

A case where the payment does fall: $280,000 at 7.4% with 25 years left (payment $2,051.00, remaining interest $335,299), refinanced to $300,000 — $20,000 cash out — at 6.0% over 30 years. The new payment is $1,798.65, saving $252.35 a month, so the $6,000 closing cost breaks even in about 23.8 months. Yet total interest rises to $347,515 — $12,216 more than staying put — because the clock restarts and the balance grew. A lower payment is not the same as a cheaper loan.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my payment go UP in the default example?

Three forces at once: the new rate (6.25%) is higher than the current one (5.9%), the balance grows by the $40,000 cash out, and only the longer 30-year term pushes back. Payment rises from $1,896 to $1,970 — and total interest jumps from $220,570 to $389,306. That is the honest answer for a lot of real cash-out quotes.

What is the true cost of $40,000 cash out?

The cash itself is not free money — it is borrowed. At 6.25% over 30 years, $40,000 of the new loan costs about $48,600 in interest on its own. A home equity loan or HELOC for the same $40,000 over a shorter term usually costs far less; compare with the HELOC vs Home Equity Loan Calculator.

When does a cash-out refinance make sense?

When the new rate is meaningfully below your current rate, the cash funds something with a return (a renovation that adds value, or retiring much higher-rate debt), and you will stay in the home well past the break-even. Run the debt case carefully — moving credit card debt to a 30-year loan lowers the payment but can raise total cost.

How much cash can I take out?

Most lenders cap the new loan at about 80% of the home's appraised value for a cash-out refinance on a primary residence. On a $450,000 home that ceiling is $360,000 total — with a $280,000 balance, up to about $80,000 of cash before closing costs.

What are typical closing costs on a refinance?

Roughly 2–5% of the new loan amount all-in, with lender fees, appraisal, title and recording making up most of it. The Closing Cost Estimator breaks the typical line items down so you can judge whether a quote's 2% is honest.

Is cash-out money taxed?

Loan proceeds are not income — you are borrowing, not earning — so the cash itself is not taxed. Whether the interest is deductible depends on use: generally it must fund improvements to the home that secures the loan. Confirm your situation with a tax professional.

Cash-out refi or a HELOC for the same money?

The refi replaces your first mortgage — worth it when its rate beats your current one — while a HELOC or home equity loan leaves the first mortgage untouched, usually the better move when your existing rate is low. The HELOC vs Home Equity Loan Calculator prices the second-lien options.
