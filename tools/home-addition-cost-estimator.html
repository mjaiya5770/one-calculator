Home Addition Cost Estimator

Addition quotes confuse because a bump-out and a second story share nothing but the word "addition". This estimator separates the five types — room, bump-out, second story, garage conversion, sunroom — each with its own per-foot band.

The default 400 sq ft is a primary-suite or family-room scale addition. Kitchens and bathrooms inside an addition carry their own premium (plumbing, cabinetry, tile) — budget them as separate projects on top of the shell figure here.

How it works

Installed per-square-foot bands by type: room addition $200–$325 (foundation, framing, roof tie-in, systems), bump-out $250–$400 (small footprints concentrate structure and roof work), second story $225–$375 (no foundation, but structural reinforcement and a roof that comes off first), garage conversion $100–$175 (the shell exists — insulation, floors, HVAC and finishes are the job), sunroom $150–$275 (kit systems at the low end, site-built at the high). Design, permits and engineering typically add 8–15% on top — inside these bands' mid-range on simple jobs, extra on complex ones.

Estimates only, typical US ranges for finished space at standard specification. Seismic, snow-load and historic requirements, and any kitchen/bath fit-out, move real quotes. Additions are the most permit- and design-sensitive project class in this network — the estimator budgets the build, not the approval process.

Method & worked example

Method: Total = addition sq ft × type band: room addition $200–325, bump-out $250–400, second story $225–375, garage conversion $100–175, sunroom $150–275 per sq ft, finished at standard specification.

Worked example: A 400 sq ft room addition: 400 × $200–325 = $80,000–$130,000, mid-band $105,000.

When to use this

  • You are weighing an addition against moving house.
  • A design-build firm quoted a per-foot number you want checked by type.
  • You are comparing building out (room) vs up (second story) vs converting the garage.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the addition's finished square footage.
  2. Pick the type that matches the structure — the band follows the type.
  3. Read the range, then budget kitchens/baths inside it separately.
  4. Add design and permits (8–15%) when moving from estimate to budget.

Another worked example

A 200 sq ft kitchen bump-out: 200 × $250–400 = $50,000–$80,000, mid-band $65,000 — before the kitchen fit-out itself. Small additions carry the highest per-foot bands in residential work because every trade still has to show up for a tiny footprint.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a 400 sq ft room addition cost?

$80,000–$130,000 finished, mid-band about $105,000. That is $200–$325 per sq ft — more per foot than new construction, because tie-ins, roof matching and systems extension are concentrated work.

How much does a 200 sq ft bump-out cost?

$50,000–$80,000. Bump-outs shock people — the per-foot band ($250–$400) is the highest of the addition types because a kitchen bump-out still needs structure, roof, and every trade for a tiny footprint.

Is it cheaper to build out or up?

Per square foot, similar: ground-level room additions run $200–$325; second stories $225–$375 but may need stair space and structural work below. Building up wins when the lot cannot grow; building out wins when the foundation and roof are simple.

Why is a garage conversion so much cheaper?

The expensive bones exist: shell, roof, slab. The $100–$175 band buys insulation, raised flooring, HVAC extension, windows and finishes. Check local rules — many towns require the parking count to survive the conversion.

Do additions pay back at resale?

Family-room and primary-suite additions historically return roughly half to two-thirds of cost at resale in national surveys; garage conversions vary by market parking pressure. The honest framing: buy the space for living in, count resale as a rebate, not the reason.

How long does an addition take?

Design and permits 2–4 months; construction 2–4 months for a room addition, longer for second stories. Weather and inspection queues stretch the calendar more than the work does.

Does an addition need its own HVAC?

Sometimes a zone or a mini-split; sometimes the existing system has capacity. The BTU Estimator sizes the added load, and the HVAC Estimator prices the equipment answer either way.
