Flooring Calculator

Flooring is sold by the box and wasted by the cut. Enter the room (or a total area), the coverage printed on the box, and your waste allowance, and get the box count and the real material cost — before the store does the math for you.

Waste guide: 5% for a plain rectangle laid straight, 10% standard, 15% for diagonal layouts, small rooms or lots of cuts. Keep one spare box after the job — a discontinued line is unmatchable later.

How it works

Area first: room length × width, or your own total for multi-room jobs. The buy quantity is area × (1 + waste %) — offcuts at walls, mistakes and pattern matching — divided by the box coverage and rounded up to whole boxes. Cost is boxes × box price; the per-sq-ft line lets you compare products sold in different box sizes honestly.

Materials estimate only. Underlayment, transitions, baseboards and adhesive are separate lines on a real quote — this counts the flooring itself.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Buy quantity = floor area × (1 + waste %) ÷ box coverage, rounded up to whole boxes; cost = boxes × box price. The waste allowance covers end cuts, mistakes and pattern matching that a bare area ÷ coverage division ignores.

Worked example: A 12 × 10 ft room is 120 sq ft; with 10% waste you buy for 132 sq ft, and at 20 sq ft per box that is 7 boxes — about $315 at $45 a box, or $2.63 per sq ft of floor. At 5% waste the count is still 7 boxes here: box rounding swallows the difference.

When to use this

  • You are standing in the flooring aisle with a room measurement and a box price, and the per-box coverage is in smaller print than either.
  • You are flooring two or three rooms on one product and need a single box count for the whole run.
  • A quote lists material by the square foot and you want to translate it into boxes and a real total before signing.
  • You are choosing between two products sold in different box sizes and need the honest per-square-foot cost of each.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the room length and width, or skip them and type a total area if you have already measured several rooms.
  2. Copy the coverage per box off the carton — not off a similar product on the next shelf.
  3. Set the waste allowance for your layout: straight, standard or diagonal.
  4. Enter the price per box.
  5. Read the boxes, the coverage you are actually buying, and the cost per square foot of finished floor.

A 15 by 14 ft living room is 210 sq ft. With a 12% waste allowance you buy for 235.2 sq ft, and at 24 sq ft per box that rounds up to 10 boxes — 240 sq ft of material — costing $580 at $58 a box, or $2.76 per square foot of floor. Frequently asked questions

How many boxes for a 12 × 10 room?

The room is 120 sq ft. With 10% waste you buy for 132 sq ft; at 20 sq ft per box that is 7 boxes — about $315 at $45 a box. Straight-lay at 5% waste would take 6.3 → also 7 boxes here, which is common: box rounding swallows small waste differences.

How much waste should I allow?

Five percent for a simple rectangle with planks run straight; ten as the safe default; fifteen for diagonal or herringbone layouts, rooms with many angles, or first-time installers. The cost of one extra box is trivial next to a mid-job stock-out in a different dye lot.

Why not just divide area by box coverage?

Because almost every row ends in a cut, and the cut-off piece only sometimes starts the next row. Real installs consume 5–15% more material than the floor’s area — the waste allowance is that reality, priced in advance.

Does the calculator work for tile or hardwood?

Yes — anything sold by coverage per box: laminate, LVP, engineered hardwood, ceramic tile. For tile with grout joints, the Tile Calculator also counts by grid, which catches edge cases a coverage count can miss.

Can I floor several rooms with one calculation?

Yes — that is what the total area field is for. Measure each room, add the areas together, and enter the sum; it overrides the length and width. Keep the waste allowance at the high end if the rooms are small or cut up, because every doorway and closet burns offcuts that a single open room would not.

What is not counted in the box total?

Underlayment, transitions, baseboards and adhesive are separate lines on a real job — this calculator counts the flooring itself and its material cost. Budget those extras alongside, and keep one spare box after the job is done: a line that gets discontinued next year cannot be matched later at any price.
