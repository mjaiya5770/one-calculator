How it works

Ovulation is driven by the luteal phase — the stretch from ovulation to the next period — which stays near 14 days for most people even when total cycle length varies. So ovulation day ≈ period start + (cycle length − 14). The fertile window opens five days earlier (sperm survival) and closes a day after ovulation (egg lifespan). If a cycle leads to pregnancy, the due-date estimate is ovulation + 266 days.

Estimates only — not medical advice and not a contraception method. Real ovulation shifts with stress, illness and irregular cycles; ovulation tests or a clinician pin it down better than any calendar.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Ovulation day = the first day of your last period + cycle length − 14 days, treating the luteal phase as a fixed 14 days. The fertile window runs from 5 days before ovulation to 1 day after; the next period is due one full cycle after the last one started, and an estimated due date is ovulation + 266 days.

Worked example: A period starting Thursday, 1 January 2026 with a 28-day cycle: ovulation falls on Thursday, 15 January, the fertile window runs 10–16 January, the next period is due 29 January, and conception in that window points to a due date of 8 October 2026.

When to use this

  • You are trying to conceive and want this cycle's best days marked, not just the textbook day 14.
  • Your cycle is longer or shorter than 28 days and you have realised ovulation moves with it.
  • You want the next three cycles laid out to plan around work, travel or treatment dates.
  • You conceived recently and want the rough due date that follows from this cycle's ovulation.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the first day of your last period — day one, not the day it ended.
  2. Set your real average cycle length, counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next.
  3. Read the ovulation day and the fertile window around it; the two days before ovulation and the day itself are the strongest.
  4. Scroll to the three-cycle table if you are planning further ahead than this month.
  5. Remember the dates are calendar estimates — tests and symptoms on the day outrank them.

Frequently asked questions

How is ovulation calculated from my period?

Ovulation happens about 14 days before the NEXT period, so with a 28-day cycle it lands on day 14, but with a 32-day cycle it lands on day 18. Enter your real average cycle length — the count matters more than the textbook 28.

What exactly is the fertile window?

The six days ending just after ovulation: sperm can live up to five days in the reproductive tract, and the egg survives roughly 24 hours. Conception is most likely in the two days before ovulation and on the day itself.

My cycles are irregular. Will this still work?

Only roughly. The calculator assumes every cycle matches your average length; irregular cycles can ovulate a week earlier or later than the estimate. For irregular cycles, treat the window as a guide and consider ovulation predictor kits.

If I conceive this cycle, when would the due date be?

About 266 days after ovulation (or 280 days after the period that started the cycle). The Pregnancy Due Date Calculator gives the full picture with trimester milestones.

Can I use this calculator to avoid pregnancy?

No, and the page says so plainly. The dates assume your cycle matches its average exactly; stress, illness and ordinary variation move real ovulation by days, and sperm survive up to five days, which widens the risky stretch well beyond the printed window. A calendar estimate is a planning aid for conceiving, not a reliable barrier to it.

Should an ovulation test result override these dates?

Yes. The calculator predicts from last cycle's pattern; an ovulation predictor kit reacts to the hormone surge in this cycle, in your body, right now. When the two disagree, trust the test and treat the printed window as the rough map it is — especially if your cycles vary by more than a couple of days.
