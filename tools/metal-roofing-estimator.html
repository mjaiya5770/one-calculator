Metal Roofing Estimator

Metal panels are ordered by the piece and cut to length — the count depends on the panel's coverage width, not its full width, and that one distinction ruins more orders than any other. Enter the building and pitch: get panels per slope, panel length, screws and ridge.

For a simple gable roof: two slopes, panels running eave to ridge. Hips and valleys need a panel layout from the supplier — and usually 10–15% more metal. Panel length shown is the slope length; add your eave overhang.

How it works

Panel count is eave length ÷ coverage width per slope — a 36-in ag panel covers 36 in, but a 16-in standing-seam panel covers 16, which is why the same 40-ft eave takes 14 ag panels or 30 seam panels per slope. Panel length is the slope length: half the span times the pitch factor √(1 + (rise/12)²). Screws for exposed-fastener panels run about 80 per square (a square = 100 sq ft of roof); standing seam hides its fasteners in clips, roughly one clip per 18 in of seam.

Estimates only. Trim — ridge cap, eave and gable trim, closures, valley metal — is sold by the piece and profile-specific; suppliers lay it out from your sketch. Panel lengths here exclude eave overhang and ridge overlap; confirm cut lengths with the supplier before ordering, since panels are non-returnable once cut.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Slope length = (span ÷ 2) × pitch factor √(1 + (rise ÷ 12)²); true roof area = length × span × factor. Panels per slope = ceil(eave length × 12 ÷ panel coverage width in inches); two slopes. Screws ≈ 80 per square (100 sq ft) for exposed-fastener panels.

Worked example: A 40 × 30 ft roof at 6/12 with 36-in panels: 14 panels per slope, 28 total at about 16.77 ft long, 13.42 squares, roughly 1,074 screws and 40 ft of ridge cap.

When to use this

  • You are ordering metal panels for a barn, shop, cabin or house gable roof.
  • You are comparing ag-panel and standing-seam quotes panel by panel.
  • You need the screw count before the fastener order goes in.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter building length (eave) and width (span).
  2. Pick the pitch from the plans or the Roof Pitch Calculator.
  3. Choose the panel system by coverage width — 36 in ag or 16/12 in seam.
  4. Order panels by count and confirmed cut length; note screws and ridge separately.

Frequently asked questions

How many metal panels do I need for a 40 × 30 roof?

At 6/12 with 36-in panels: 14 panels per slope, 28 total, each about 16.77 ft long (half the 30-ft span, pitched). The roof is 13.42 squares and takes roughly 1,074 exposed-fastener screws.

How long are the panels on a 30 × 24 roof at 4/12?

The slope runs 12.65 ft (half of 24 ft × the 1.054 factor), so panels cut around 12.7 ft plus overhang. That roof takes 10 panels per slope in 36-in coverage — 20 total.

Standing seam or exposed fastener?

Exposed-fastener (ag) panels are cheaper and DIY-friendly, but their screws have rubber washers that age in 15–20 years. Standing seam hides every fastener, costs roughly double in material, and is the 40-year answer. Panel count differs because coverage widths differ — compare installed totals, not panel prices.

How many screws per square?

Plan on about 80 screws per square for exposed-fastener panels over purlins or deck, per typical fastening patterns — more in high-wind zones. They come in bags of 250; the estimator's count rounds your order sensibly.

Can metal go over old shingles?

Often yes — over one layer, ideally on furring strips — which saves the tear-off. Check local code and the panel maker's spec, and weigh the saving against the chance to inspect and re-deck.

What gauge metal should I buy?

29-gauge is the agricultural standard and the price leader; 26-gauge is the residential upgrade that resists hail dents and foot traffic better. Standing seam usually runs 24–26 gauge. Panel count does not change — price and lifespan do.

Do I need underlayment under metal?

Yes — high-temperature ice-and-water or synthetic underlayment over the deck (or over purlins per the panel spec). It is also the condensation management layer, which matters more on metal than on shingles.
