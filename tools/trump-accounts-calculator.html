Trump Accounts Calculator

A Trump Account is a new kind of IRA for a child, created by the 2025 tax law: the federal government adds a one-time $1,000 for eligible children born in 2025–2028, family and employers can add up to $5,000 a year combined, and the money stays locked until the child turns 18. Enter a birth year and a contribution plan and see what it could grow to — every rule below is sourced from IRS guidance, and the parts that are not final yet are labelled as such.

The law caps family + employer contributions at $5,000 a year combined (employer part capped at $2,500) — enter more and the calculator models the capped amount and tells you. The projection runs from birth to the 18th birthday; real accounts opened later simply have fewer years, and the seed is invested when the account is established, not literally on the birthday.

How it works

Trump Accounts were created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025; Internal Revenue Code §530A) and explained in IRS Notice 2025-68. The verified rules this calculator models: any child under 18 with a Social Security number can have an account, opened by a parent or guardian election (IRS Form 4547, or trumpaccounts.gov); a one-time $1,000 federal pilot contribution goes to children who are U.S. citizens born January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2028; family, friends and employers may contribute up to $5,000 a year combined (indexed for inflation after 2027), with the employer share capped at $2,500 per employee per year and tax-free to the employee — the $1,000 pilot does not count against the $5,000; contributions could first be made from July 4, 2026; and money generally cannot be withdrawn before January 1 of the year the child turns 18, after which the account is treated like a traditional IRA. Not yet final: the detailed regulations were still in proposed form in 2026, and program mechanics (exact deposit timing, state/charity top-up programs) can still change — check trumpaccounts.gov for the current state. The math here is a plain compound-growth projection: the seed plus each year’s contributions, grown at the return you assume. Nothing here is guaranteed — markets do not pay a fixed rate.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Balance at 18 = seed × (1 + r)years + (family + employer) × [((1 + r)years − 1) ÷ r] × (1 + r), where r is the assumed annual return and the contributions are modelled at the start of each year. The seed is $1,000 for an eligible child (U.S. citizen born 2025–2028, SSN, election filed) and counts from birth; years = 18 − (2026 − birth year), so a later birth year means fewer compounding years. Family + employer contributions are capped at $5,000 a year combined, the employer share at $2,500.

Worked example: A child born in 2026, the $1,000 seed, $1,000 a year from family and a 6% assumed return: the account reaches $35,614.33 at age 18 — $19,000 of contributions and $16,614.33 of growth. The year-by-year table shows the balance passing $10,000 at age 7 and growth nearly catching total contributions by 18, which is compounding doing the heavy lifting late.

When to use this

  • Your child was born in 2025–2028 and you want to know what the free $1,000 actually becomes by 18 — with and without your own contributions on top.
  • Your employer offers a Trump Account contribution program and you are deciding whether to route family money through it for the tax-free $2,500.
  • You are comparing a Trump Account against a 529 or a custodial brokerage account and want the Trump Account's own trajectory first.
  • Grandparents ask what a yearly birthday contribution would grow to — this shows them the number without a sales pitch.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the child's birth year — the pilot eligibility line updates from the published 2025–2028 window.
  2. Set the seed drop-down: let it check the birth year, or force Yes/No if you know the child's citizenship or filing situation differs.
  3. Enter what family and others will contribute each year, and any employer contribution separately (it has its own $2,500 cap).
  4. Set the assumed return — 6% is a middle case, not a forecast — and a savings-account rate for the comparison.
  5. Read the balance at 18 with the contributed-versus-growth split, then scan the year-by-year table to see when compounding takes over.

Frequently asked questions

Which children get the $1,000?

U.S. citizens born between January 1, 2025 and December 31, 2028 who have a Social Security number — with a parent or guardian filing the election (IRS Form 4547 or trumpaccounts.gov). There is no income test. Children born outside that window can still have a Trump Account; they just do not receive the federal $1,000.

How much can our family add each year?

Up to $5,000 a year in total from family, friends and employers combined. An employer may contribute up to $2,500 of that per employee per year, tax-free to the employee, under a formal employer program. The federal $1,000 pilot contribution sits outside the cap. On the defaults here — a 2026-born child, $1,000 a year from family at 6% — the account reaches $35,614.33 by age 18: $19,000 contributed, $16,614.33 of growth.

When can the money come out?

Generally not before January 1 of the calendar year the child turns 18 — that lock is the design, not a bug. After the growth period the account is generally treated as a traditional IRA, with the usual IRA tax rules on withdrawals. Narrow exceptions exist in the guidance; your own situation belongs with the IRS pages or a tax professional, not a calculator.

Can I open one today, and where?

Elections opened with 2025 tax returns (Form 4547) and at trumpaccounts.gov; contributions themselves could first be made from July 4, 2026. The Treasury establishes the initial account; rollover accounts at financial institutions follow once the initial account exists. Only one funded Trump Account is allowed per child.

Is 6% a promise?

No — it is an assumption you control. Trump Accounts must be invested in diversified index-style investments during the growth period, and long-run stock-market averages sit above 6%, but any single 18-year stretch can land well above or below it. Try 4% and 8% as well: at $1,000 a year the spread between them is the difference between a solid head start and a large one.

Does the $1,000 count toward the $5,000 yearly cap?

No. The pilot contribution sits outside the cap — the $5,000 limit covers family, friends and employer money combined. It is also why the calculator adds the seed separately from the annual contribution lines: the seed is a one-time deposit, not part of any year's $5,000.

What if my child was born before 2025?

They can still have a Trump Account — any child under 18 with a Social Security number can — but there is no federal $1,000, and the growth window is shorter. A child born in 2020 with $1,000 a year going in at 6% has 12 years of growth and reaches $17,882.14 by 18: $12,000 contributed, $5,882.14 of growth.
