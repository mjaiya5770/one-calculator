How it works

Each month the calculator adds one month of interest (balance × APR ÷ 12), then subtracts your payment. It repeats until the balance hits zero, counting the months. That month-by-month simulation is exactly how credit card statements accrue interest, so the result mirrors real life.

Estimates only — not financial advice. Actual results vary with rate changes, fees, and new charges.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Each month the balance grows by APR ÷ 12, then your payment comes off: balance × (1 + APR ÷ 12) − payment, repeated until it reaches zero.

Worked example: A $3,000 balance at 18% APR paid at $120/month clears in 32 months, with about $788 paid in interest along the way.

When to use this

  • One balance, one APR and one fixed amount you can pay — you want the debt-free date, not a guess.
  • A card has been cut up and you are deciding what autopay amount actually ends it.
  • You are comparing paying the balance off faster against a balance-transfer offer with a deadline.
  • Minimum payments have stopped making sense and you want to see what a fixed payment does instead.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Enter the current balance exactly as the statement shows it.
  2. Enter the APR — the purchase APR if the card has several.
  3. Enter the fixed monthly payment you will make every month, not the minimum.
  4. Add any extra on top in the extra field and watch the months and interest fall.
  5. If the tool warns the payment does not cover the monthly interest, raise the payment until the warning clears — below that line the balance grows forever.

Another worked example

A $5,200 balance at 23.99% APR, paid at $200 a month plus $80 extra ($280 in total): the debt clears in 24 months with $1,364.98 of interest and $6,564.98 paid overall. At the $200 alone it takes 38 months and $2,211.45 of interest, so the extra $80 buys debt freedom 14 months sooner and saves $846.47.

Frequently asked questions

Debt avalanche vs. debt snowball — which is better?

The avalanche (pay highest APR first) costs the least in interest. The snowball (pay smallest balance first) gives faster wins that keep you motivated. Both beat minimums — pick the one you'll stick with.

Why do minimum payments take forever?

Minimums are often 1–2% of the balance, barely covering interest. On an $8,000 balance at 20% APR, a $160 minimum can take 7+ years and cost over $5,000 in interest. Raise the payment above to see the difference.

Does an extra $50 a month really help?

Enormously. Extra payments go 100% to principal, which shrinks every future interest charge. Try adding $50 or $100 in the extra-payment field and watch months and interest collapse.

What happens if my payment does not cover the monthly interest?

The balance grows instead of shrinking — each month adds more interest than you pay off, and the debt never ends. This calculator flags that case with a warning rather than inventing a payoff date. The fix is mechanical: raise the payment above the monthly interest line (balance times APR divided by 12) and the countdown starts.

Should I use this for a car loan or another fixed-term loan?

Not really. This tool assumes an open-ended balance where you choose the payment, like a credit card. A car loan already has its payoff date built into its term — what you want there is the effect of paying extra, which the Amortization Calculator and the Car Loan Calculator model with the loan's actual schedule.

How exact is the debt-free date?

It assumes the APR never changes, no new charges land on the balance, and every payment arrives on time. Real cards break all three assumptions occasionally — a rate rise or one emergency charge pushes the date out. Treat the month shown as the plan if nothing changes, and re-run it whenever the balance or rate moves.
