How it works

The math is simple: rate = (target income + annual expenses) ÷ annual billable hours × (1 + profit margin). Annual billable hours = billable hours per week × working weeks per year. The day rate is the hourly rate × 8, handy for project quotes.

Estimates only — rates also depend on your market, niche, and demand. This gives you the floor you should never price below.

Formula & worked example

Formula: Billable hours = hours per week × weeks worked per year. Break-even rate = (target income + business expenses) ÷ billable hours. Your chargeable rate then adds the profit margin: break-even rate × (1 + margin %).

Worked example: Target $80,000 with $6,000 of expenses, billing 25 hours a week for 48 weeks — 1,200 billable hours. Break-even is $86,000 ÷ 1,200 = $71.67 an hour; add a 20% margin and the rate is $86.00 an hour, a $688 day rate.

When to use this

  • You are leaving employment to freelance and need the rate that replaces your salary, not a guess copied from a job board.
  • A client asks for a day rate and you need one derived from your actual costs and hours.
  • Your current rate keeps you busy but the income never arrives — you suspect the billable-hours assumption is the lie.
  • You are cutting to a four-day week or fewer clients and need the rate that makes the reduced schedule pay the same.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Set the annual income you want the freelance work itself to produce.
  2. Add every annual business expense — software, gear, insurance, accounting, workspace.
  3. Enter billable hours per week honestly: only hours a client pays for.
  4. Enter the weeks you will actually work after holidays, sick time and quiet spells.
  5. Choose a profit margin so slow months and savings come out of the rate, not out of you.
  6. Treat the hourly and day rates as a floor: quote at or above them, never below to win work.

Another worked example

A designer targeting $65,000 with $4,800 of annual business expenses, billing 20 hours a week for 46 weeks, has 920 billable hours and $69,800 to cover. The break-even rate is $75.87 an hour; add a 15% profit margin and the rate to charge is $87.25 an hour — a $698.00 day rate. Quoting $60 an hour to be competitive would quietly hand back a large slice of the target income.

Frequently asked questions

Why aren't my billable hours 40 per week?

Almost no freelancer bills 40 hours. Admin, marketing, proposals, bookkeeping, and downtime typically eat 30–50% of the week. Most freelancers bill 20–30 hours — 25 is a safe default.

When should I raise my rates?

Review yearly or whenever demand is steady and you're booked out. If more than ~70% of proposals close, you're likely underpriced. Raising rates 5–10% annually keeps up with costs.

What expenses should I include?

Everything the business costs you: software subscriptions, hardware, internet, phone, insurance, accounting, coworking or home-office costs, travel, and self-employment tax buffer. Don't forget to pay yourself for non-billable work.

Do I still need to set money aside for tax on top of this rate?

Yes — this calculation does not include a tax line. The target income you enter is what the business must generate, and self-employment tax plus income tax still have to come out of what you earn. The clean way to handle it inside this tool is to add your expected tax reserve to annual expenses, or raise the target income by the amount you plan to set aside, so the rate carries the tax bill instead of your savings absorbing it.

What if clients will not pay the rate this produces?

Then something else in the equation has to move, because the arithmetic will not: more billable hours, lower expenses, a lower income target, or a different market — scarcer skills and clients with expensive problems pay rates generalists cannot. What does not work is charging the lower rate and hoping volume fixes it; below break-even, every extra hour of work deepens the hole at a faster rate.

Should rush jobs and specialist work use this same rate?

No — this rate is your floor for ordinary work on ordinary timelines. Rush deadlines, scarce expertise and work that directly makes or saves a client serious money are all reasons to price above the floor, and project or value pricing often beats hourly billing there entirely. The floor exists so that enthusiasm for an interesting job never quietly turns it into a loss.
