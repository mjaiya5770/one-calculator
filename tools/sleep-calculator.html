Sleep Calculator

Waking up groggy is often a timing problem, not a quantity problem. Sleep runs in 90-minute cycles, and waking at the end of one feels radically better than waking mid-cycle. Tell it when you must wake up — or when you plan to sleep — and it counts backwards or forwards in whole cycles.

We count back in 90-minute cycles and subtract 14 minutes to fall asleep — so these are lights-out times, not get-into-bed times.
We add 14 minutes to fall asleep, then count forward in 90-minute cycles — set your alarm for one of these.

How it works

A full sleep cycle — light sleep, deep sleep, dreaming — lasts about 90 minutes, and the average person takes about 14 minutes to fall asleep. So the math is: target time ∓ (cycles × 90 minutes) ∓ 14 minutes. Five cycles (7½ hours of sleep) suits most adults; six cycles (9 hours) is the full tank. The suggestions are ordered with the recommended 5–6 cycle options first.

A planning aid, not medical advice. Cycle length varies by person and by night (85–110 minutes is the normal range), and persistent exhaustion deserves a clinician, not a calculator.

Method & worked example

Method: Sleep runs in roughly 90-minute cycles, and falling asleep takes about 14 minutes on average. Bedtimes = wake time − (cycles × 90 minutes) − 14 minutes; wake times = bedtime + 14 minutes + (cycles × 90 minutes). Five or six cycles suit most adults.

Worked example: To wake at 6:30 AM: six cycles means lights out at 9:16 PM; five cycles — the sweet spot for most adults — means 10:46 PM. Going to bed at 11:00 PM instead, the matching wake times are 8:14 AM (six cycles) and 6:44 AM (five).

When to use this

  • An early start is fixed — a shift, a flight, an exam — and the bedtime has to be worked backwards from it.
  • You sleep a full eight hours and still wake up feeling flattened, and you suspect the timing rather than the amount.
  • You fell asleep on the sofa at an odd hour and need an alarm time that does not cut a cycle in half.
  • You are resetting your schedule after travel and want the first night planned instead of guessed.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Pick the mode that matches what you know: the wake-up time, or the bedtime.
  2. Enter that one time exactly as your alarm or your lights-out is set.
  3. Look at the five-cycle option first — it is the tool's headline recommendation for most adults.
  4. Compare the six-cycle option if you can afford the earlier bedtime or the later alarm.
  5. If you know you take longer than 14 minutes to drift off, move the suggested time earlier by the difference.

With the alarm fixed at 7:15 AM, the tool counts back in whole cycles: six cycles means lights out at 10:01 PM, and five cycles — the headline recommendation — means 11:31 PM, for 7.5 hours of actual sleep. Leave it too late and the remaining whole-cycle options are 1:01 AM for four cycles and 2:31 AM for three, which is the arithmetic behind the familiar feeling that a short night planned around cycles beats a longer one that ends mid-cycle.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I go to bed if I wake up at 6:30 AM?

For six full cycles: lights out at 9:16 PM. For five cycles — the sweet spot for most adults — 10:46 PM. Four cycles means 12:16 AM and noticeably less deep sleep. Each option above includes the 14 minutes it takes to drift off.

I am going to bed at 11 PM — when should I set my alarm?

After five cycles: 6:44 AM. After six: 8:14 AM. Waking at, say, 7:00 instead slices into the middle of a cycle, which is why 7 can feel worse than 6:44 despite the extra minutes.

How many cycles do I need?

Most adults do best on 5–6 cycles (7½–9 hours of actual sleep). Teenagers need more; some adults genuinely thrive on the lower end. If you wake naturally before your alarm most days, you are probably landing at a cycle boundary already.

Why 14 minutes to fall asleep?

It is the widely used average sleep-onset latency for healthy adults. If you know you take longer — 30 minutes is common with screens in bed — shift every suggested time earlier by the difference.

Can I use this to plan a nap?

Partly. The same cycle logic says a nap of one full cycle is about 90 minutes of sleep, plus the time you take to drift off — very different from the 20-minute power nap, which works precisely because you wake before deep sleep starts. What this tool does not do is tell you which kind of nap your afternoon needs; if a 90-minute nap leaves you groggy anyway, the shorter kind is usually the fix.

Why might a cycle-boundary wake-up still feel rough?

Because 90 minutes is an average. Real cycles run anywhere from about 85 to 110 minutes and change length across the night, so a boundary calculated to the minute is still an estimate. Sleep debt, alcohol the night before and an inconsistent wake time all blunt the benefit too — the calculator fixes the timing variable, not the others.
