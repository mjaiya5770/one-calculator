How to Get Out of Debt: The Avalanche vs Snowball Playbook

Avalanche or snowball? A worked playbook for getting out of debt with the same $500 a month — real payoff dates, real interest totals, and how to pick the method you will actually finish.

Guide · Published November 8, 2026 · One Calculator

Two methods, one set of rules

Every serious debt-payoff plan — avalanche, snowball, or anything else — is built from the same three rules. List every debt you owe, with its balance, its interest rate and its minimum payment. Pay every minimum, on time, every month, so nothing falls behind. Then aim all of your spare cash at ONE target debt until it is gone, and roll what you were paying on it into the next target.

The two famous methods differ in exactly one decision: how you choose the target.

The avalanche: highest interest rate first

The debt avalanche targets the debt with the highest APR, regardless of size. The logic is pure arithmetic: the most expensive debt is the one growing fastest against you, so killing it first minimises the total interest you will ever pay. If you finish the plan, the avalanche is never worse than any other order on cost — usually it is the cheapest route out.

Its weakness is patience. If your highest-rate debt is also a big one, the first victory can be many months away, and a lot of people quietly quit a plan that never gives them a win.

The snowball: smallest balance first

The debt snowball targets the smallest balance, regardless of rate. A small debt can be wiped out in a month or two, and that early, complete victory — one fewer bill, forever — is powerful motivation. Each cleared debt also frees its minimum payment sooner, so the amount rolling into the next target grows like, well, a snowball.

Its cost is real but often exaggerated: because a cheap small debt may be cleared while an expensive big one keeps accruing, the snowball usually pays somewhat more interest than the avalanche. The example below shows how "somewhat" it can be.

The same $500 a month, both ways

Meet a household with three debts and $500 a month to throw at them:

Store financing$4504.9%$25
Credit card$2,40019.99%$60
Personal loan$6,0008%$122

The minimums total $207. The household adds $293 of spare cash, so $500 a month goes to debt in every scenario. Only the aim changes.

PlanDebt-free inTotal interestFirst debt gone
Minimums only ($207/mo)~67 months~$2,900—
Avalanche ($500/mo)20 months$699Credit card, month 8
Snowball ($500/mo)20 months$738Store financing, month 2

Read that table twice, because it carries the whole lesson. Both methods finish in the same month. The snowball costs about $39 more in interest — the price of a pizza night — and in exchange it delivers a completely paid-off debt in month 2 instead of month 8, and clears the credit card in month 9. Meanwhile the real catastrophe in the table is the first row: paying only minimums takes over five years and roughly $2,900 of interest. Committing the extra $293 matters ten times more than which order you pick.

Which one should you pick?

Pick the avalanche if the numbers themselves motivate you, your highest-rate debt is small enough to fall within a few months, or the interest gap in your situation is large (run both orders with your real balances — with big card balances at high rates, the gap can reach hundreds of dollars, and then the math deserves a vote).

Pick the snowball if you have tried and stalled before, if you carry several small debts that each feel like a separate emergency, or if an early win is what will keep the $500 showing up month after month. A hybrid is legitimate too: snowball one small debt for the early win, then switch to avalanche order for the rest.

The honest verdict: the best method is the one you finish. A "perfect" plan abandoned in month three loses to an "inefficient" plan that runs for twenty.

The playbook: five steps to start this week

  1. List it all. Every balance, APR and minimum, including the awkward ones. The plan only works on the full picture.
  2. Protect the floor. Keep a small starter emergency fund — even $500–$1,000 — so one car repair doesn't go straight back onto a card.
  3. Pick your order. Avalanche or snowball, using the rule above. Write the order down.
  4. Automate the minimums, aim the extra. Minimums on autopay for everything; the spare cash goes to the target debt only.
  5. Model each debt. Our Debt Payoff Calculator handles one debt at a time — balance, APR, payment and extra payment — and shows your debt-free date and total interest. Run each of your debts through it, then stack the results in your chosen order. For a card balance specifically, the Credit Card Payoff Calculator shows how the payoff time collapses as the payment rises.

After the last payment

The month the final debt dies, you have a trained $500-a-month habit and nothing to aim it at. Aim it at savings before your lifestyle notices it is free: the same discipline that cleared the debts builds an emergency fund remarkably fast. Frequently asked questions

Is the avalanche always better?

On cost, yes — it never pays more interest than another order with the same monthly total. But "better" includes finishing, and in worked examples like the one above the snowball's extra cost can be as little as a few dozen dollars for a much earlier first win.

Can I mix both methods?

Yes. A common hybrid: clear one small debt snowball-style for momentum, then switch to avalanche order (highest APR first) for everything that remains.

Should I stop saving completely while I pay off debt?

Keep a small starter emergency fund first. Without one, the next unexpected bill goes back on a card and quietly restarts the cycle the plan is trying to end.
