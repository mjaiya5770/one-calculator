Compound Interest, Explained With Real Numbers

Compound interest without the hand-waving: exactly how $10,000 and $200 a month grow over 10, 20 and 30 years, why starting five years earlier beats saving more, and the rule of 72.

Guide · Published November 8, 2026 · One Calculator

The one-sentence idea

Compound interest means you earn interest on your interest — so growth doesn't move in a straight line, it curves upward, slowly at first and then startlingly fast.

The small version: put $1,000 at 7% for two years. With simple interest you earn $70 each year and finish with $1,140. With compound interest, year one still adds $70 — but year two pays 7% on $1,070, adding $74.90, for a finish of $1,144.90. That extra $4.90 is interest on interest. It looks trivial. Given decades, it becomes the main event.

The formula, briefly

For a lump sum, the future value is A = P(1 + r)t — A is the final amount, P the starting principal, r the yearly rate as a decimal (7% = 0.07), and t the number of years. Everything in this article is that one line, applied patiently. Monthly deposits follow the same idea; each deposit simply gets its own, shorter run of compounding.

What 7% does to $10,000

Here is a single $10,000 lump sum at a steady 7% per year. Treat these as illustrations of the mechanism, not predictions — real markets bounce around year to year, even when their long-run average lands near a number like this.

10$19,671.51$9,671.51
20$38,696.84$28,696.84
30$76,122.55$66,122.55

Look at the shape, not just the totals. The first decade adds about $9,672. The second adds about $19,025. The third adds about $37,426 — more than the first two decades combined. Nothing changed except the balance the 7% gets applied to. That is the bend in the curve, and it is why the early years feel disappointing and the late years feel like magic.

What 7% does to $200 a month

Most people don't have a lump sum; they have a monthly amount. Save $200 a month for 30 years at 7% (compounded monthly) and the same curve appears in a friendlier costume: you deposit $72,000 of your own money and finish with about $243,994. Roughly $172,000 of the ending balance is interest — money your money earned while you weren't looking.

The rule of 72

For a quick estimate of doubling time, divide 72 by the annual rate. At 7%, 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3 years to double — the exact answer is 10.24 years, close enough for any conversation. At 3% (a decent savings account in a good year), money doubles in about 24 years. At credit-card rates around 24%, a balance you never paid would double in about 3. The rule works at every scale, which is precisely the warning in the next section.

Time beats amount

Because the last decade does the heavy lifting, starting earlier usually beats saving more. A deposit made at 25 gets one full extra doubling (at 7%) compared with the same deposit made at 35 — it is worth twice as much at 65 without a single extra dollar added. This is why "I'll start when I earn more" is such an expensive sentence: the years you skip are the ones that would have compounded the longest. The dark side: it compounds against you too

The formula does not care whether the balance is an asset or a debt. Carry a credit-card balance and the interest is added to what you owe; next month you are charged interest on the interest. Minimum payments are calibrated to keep that curve gentle-looking while it runs against you for years. Compound interest is a wonderful servant and a genuinely terrible master, and the only difference is which side of the balance you stand on.

Does compounding frequency matter?

A little. Interest can be applied yearly, monthly or daily; the more often it is applied, the slightly sooner each scrap of interest starts earning its own interest. At the same stated rate, daily compounding ends a touch ahead of monthly, which ends a touch ahead of annual. But the effect is small next to the two levers that actually move outcomes: the rate, and — above all — the time you give it. Choose accounts and debts by rate and fees first; let frequency be a tiebreaker.

Every figure in this article uses a steady 7% to make the mechanism visible. Real investing returns arrive unevenly — down years included — which is an argument for starting early and staying boring, not for expecting a straight line.

Frequently asked questions

Is 7% a realistic return?

It is a common long-run illustration for diversified stock-market investing, not a guarantee and not any single year's result. Savings accounts pay far less; the rate you use should match the thing you are actually modelling.

How often should interest compound to matter?

Frequency is a small effect next to rate and time. Daily or monthly compounding nudges the total up slightly versus annual, but an extra decade of growth outweighs any frequency difference.

Does compound interest apply to my savings account?

Yes — at whatever rate the account pays. The mechanics are identical to the examples above; only the rate (and therefore the doubling time) changes.
