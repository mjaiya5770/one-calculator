CD vs Savings Account: The APY Math, Honestly Compared

A CD pays more because your money is locked; a savings account pays less because it is not. The exact gap on $10,000 over one, three and five years, what the early-withdrawal penalty really costs, and the ladder that splits the difference.

Blog · Published November 8, 2026 · One Calculator

We get this question constantly: should I keep cash in a CD or a high-yield savings account? The answer is not a personality quiz. It is a trade between certainty and flexibility, and the APY math shows exactly what you pay for each side.

The one-line trade

A CD is a promise. You leave the money for the full term, and the bank pays a higher, fixed rate. A savings account — including a high-yield one — can change its rate any time, and it lets you leave whenever you want. That is the entire deal in one sentence.

CDs reward you for giving up access. Savings accounts charge you a lower yield in exchange for keeping your escape hatch open. Every other detail in this post is just the math underneath that sentence.

APY literacy in one short section

APY already includes compounding. That means when you compare a 5.00% APY CD to a 4.25% APY high-yield savings account, the compounding frequency is already inside both numbers. You do not need to adjust for daily, monthly, or quarterly compounding. The APY comparison is the whole comparison.

This saves you from a common mistake: seeing a nominal rate and trying to guess how often interest is credited. If the label says APY, the compounding is baked in. Compare APY to APY and move on.

The penalty, priced

CDs usually charge an early withdrawal penalty if you take the money before the term ends. That penalty typically costs 3 to 12 months of interest.

On the example CD, six months of interest is $250. The entire first-year APY gap over the savings account is only $75. So if you break the CD inside roughly the first year, the penalty is more than three times the extra interest you earned by choosing the CD. The “better rate” lost.

Before opening a CD, price the penalty in dollars. If six months of interest is $250 and the rate edge is $75 a year, you need more than a year untouched just to make the penalty irrelevant.

This is why CDs are not a trick for earning more on money you might need. The penalty is the price of the higher fixed rate, and it is collected exactly when you need the cash.

The rate-risk mirror

A CD’s fixed rate protects you if rates fall. You keep the 5.00% APY for the whole term even if high-yield savings drops to 3.00% or 2.00%. That certainty is valuable when the rate cycle turns down.

The mirror image is also true: if rates rise, the CD strands you at the old lower rate. You watch new CDs and savings accounts pay more while your money is locked. A savings account floats both ways — you get the lower APY today, but you also get every future increase without moving your money.

This is not a reason to avoid CDs. It is a reason to know which risk you are choosing. A fixed rate is a bet that you value predictability more than the chance to chase rising yields.

The ladder: a yearly escape hatch

You can soften the CD’s lock-up by splitting money across 1, 2, and 3-year CDs. When the 1-year CD matures, you get a slice back with interest. You can spend it, move it to a high-yield savings account, or roll it into a new 3-year CD.

Every year after that, one slice matures. The ladder gives you CD certainty with a yearly escape hatch. You still earn fixed rates on the longer rungs, but you never have all your cash stuck behind a single early withdrawal penalty.

The inflation footnote

Both CDs and savings accounts have the same silent enemy: inflation. A 5% APY against 3% inflation is a 2% real return. That is not a small detail. It means your cash is still losing purchasing power if inflation runs above the rate you are paid.

Judge both homes for cash in real terms, not just by the number on the statement. A 4.25% high-yield savings APY against 3% inflation is a 1.25% real return. A 5% CD against 3% inflation is a 2% real return. Both beat a checking account near zero, but neither is a wealth-building machine.

The CD-versus-savings decision is not about which product is “better.” It is about whether you want the bank to pay you for giving up access or pay you less to keep access. Once you know the date you will need the money, the APY table does the rest.

Frequently asked questions

Is money in a CD safe?

Bank deposits carry FDIC insurance up to the standard limits, so the risk in a CD is not loss of principal at an insured bank. The real risks are the early-withdrawal penalty and rate regret if rates move after you lock in.

Can a savings account rate drop?

Yes. A savings account rate can change at any time. That flexibility is exactly what the lower APY pays for compared to a fixed-rate CD.

When does the CD clearly win?

Money with a known date at least a year away that you will not touch. A house deposit on a fixed timeline is the classic case because the term matches the need and the penalty is not a factor if you hold to maturity.
